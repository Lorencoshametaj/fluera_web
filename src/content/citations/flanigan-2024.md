Twenty-four studies, twenty-one articles, roughly three thousand college students. The question: does it matter whether lecture notes are typed or handwritten?

The answer is smaller than the folklore and more useful than the folklore. Typing wins decisively on volume — typed notes contain far more words (Hedges' g = 0.919). Handwriting wins on what the notes are *for*: achievement (g = 0.248, p < 0.001). Three moderators were tested. Test delay did not matter. Assessment type — factual or conceptual — did not matter. The third one did: **the opportunity to review the notes before the assessment made the handwriting advantage larger, not smaller.**

That last finding is the one worth building on. It says the value of handwritten notes is not spent at the moment of writing; it is realised later, when you come back to them.

## Why this replaces Mueller & Oppenheimer (2014)

For a decade the standard citation for "write by hand" was Mueller & Oppenheimer's *The Pen Is Mightier Than the Keyboard*, which reported a large conceptual-recall advantage for longhand. That specific study did not survive replication: Morehead, Dunlosky & Rawson (2019) failed to reproduce the effect, and Urry et al. (2021) ran a direct replication plus a mini meta-analysis across eight related studies and found essentially nothing.

The two results are not in conflict. An effect of g ≈ 0.25 is simply too small for a single replication with 142 participants to detect. Urry did not show that the effect is absent; it showed that one study could not carry it.

So the finding stands and the source changes. We cite the meta-analysis, with its real effect size, because a claim is only as strong as the evidence a reader finds when they go looking.

## What this does not license

It does not license "handwriting rewires your brain", "learning styles", or a percentage added to anyone's grade. The binomial display in the paper corresponds to roughly 9.5% of handwriters reaching an A against 6% of typists — real, and modest. The studies cover college students, and they do not include students with disabilities, so no accessibility claim rests here.
